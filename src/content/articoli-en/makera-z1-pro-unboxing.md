The Makera Z1 Pro has arrived. It's my first CNC, and I opened it on camera: this article lists
**everything that was in the box**, package by package, with what each part is for explained for
people coming from 3D printing. The machine doesn't get switched on here: the milling tests come in
the next videos.

> **Disclosure:** I paid for the Z1 Pro myself, as a Kickstarter backer. The **PCB Fab Kit** you'll
> see further down, on the other hand, is something I asked Makera for as support for my PCB content,
> and they included it. Makera didn't see the video before it went out and didn't ask me to say
> anything. I also asked them for a discount code, which you'll find at the end.

## 76 days in transit

I backed the Z1 Pro on Kickstarter among the last, on 8 May. Shipping took far longer than planned:
in August it was loaded onto one of the largest container ships in the world, which came back to
its departure port two days later because of typhoons. The shortest route to Europe was closed, so
the ship sailed around Africa to Rotterdam, and the parcel reached Italy from there. All in all,
**76 days of shipping**.

If you have a Z1 on the way and it feels late, that's almost always why: the shipping routes, not
Makera.

## How it's packed

Well, and I say that without reservations. Moulded foam, each accessory in its own box, silica gel
against moisture, and the quick start guide even explains **how to lift the machine out of the
box**: not a small detail with a machine of over 20 kg. The parcel is big, bulky and above all heavy.
If you can, open it with a second person.

On top of the machine you'll find the contents list, a safety guide, the quick start guide and some
stickers.

## The machine

If you come from 3D printing, the first thing that strikes you is the weight. A printer, even a big
one, is light by comparison: the Z1 Pro is **die-cast aluminium**, and as soon as you lift it you know
you're dealing with something built to work.

- **20 × 20 cm work bed**, with parts up to 10 cm tall.
- **Spindle with a release lever**: push the lever down, pull the bit, put another one in. Swapping is
  very quick.
- Next to the spindle sits **the probe sensor**, used to measure the bed and the stock.
- The **front opens up**, so you can machine parts longer than the bed.
- At the back: power socket, power switch and **a row of accessory ports**, including the one for the
  emergency stop button and the one for **direct chip extraction**.

Build quality, to the eye and to the touch, is excellent. How much that matters, the milling will
tell.

## The tool kit (what everyone gets)

This is the box that ships with every Z1, including one you buy today. Inside:

- Aluminium **clamps**, with screws that slot into the bed. They hold the stock down.
- A **wrench** and a **screwdriver**.
- A **multi-tool** with files and a small saw: it's for cutting off the tab that stays attached when
  you cut a part out of a sheet.
- Two **hex drivers** for the clamp screws.
- **Six bits** as a first starter set, each with a printed code that tells you what it's for.
- The **collars** and the black tool used to fit them: you slide the collar onto the bit until it
  bottoms out, so the bit sticks out of the spindle by exactly the right length.

Workholding is the first thing that changes compared to 3D printing. A print sits on the bed and
stays there. Here the bit spins, and spins fast: **if the stock isn't clamped properly it gets thrown
around inside the machine**, and you can break the bit, the part or both.

## Materials and accessories

In the materials and accessories box:

- Safety **glasses**, a **spare probe rod** and a small box with the probe and **spare tips**.
- **Double-sided tape**, for holding thin stock when you're only working the surface.
- A **USB-A to USB-C cable** and the **power cable**.
- The **emergency stop button**, which plugs into one of the ports at the back. Fit it before anything
  else.
- **Two lubricants**: grease and oil.
- A **spare limit switch**.
- A **very fine file** for cleaning machining residue off the part.
- The **6 mm collet** and **two 6 to 3.175 mm adapters**. 3.175 mm is an eighth of an inch, the shank
  size of most bits you buy online.

Plus a small **materials kit** for the first tests:

- two sheets of **two-tone plastic**, with a silver top layer on one and gold on the other over black:
  engrave through it and you get high-contrast text and graphics;
- a **10 × 10 cm MDF** square;
- a **10 × 10 cm aluminium plate, 1 cm thick**, for your first metal parts;
- **two pieces of epoxy resin**.

### What was a Kickstarter gift

Some of what I found were **rewards tied to the campaign's stretch goals**: the two MDF spoil boards,
the spare probe rod, the 6 mm collet with the two adapters, plus a thank-you card. **If you buy a Z1
today, these extras aren't included**: the box holds the machine and the tool kit. Keep that in mind
when you watch a backer's unboxing and do your sums.

## Spoil boards, explained for 3D printing people

Two MDF panels that sit on the bed. The name means nothing if you come from 3D printing, so here's
what they're for.

When the cutter finishes cutting a part, it **goes a little below the material** to free it
completely. If the aluminium bed is right underneath, it gets cut. The spoil board is a **sacrificial
panel**: it gets damaged instead of the machine, and when it's worn out you throw it away and put in
a new one. It lasts a great many jobs.

I also found a file to 3D print them, and I'll try it: if it works, it's a consumable you can make
yourself.

## The PCB Fab Kit

This is the part I care about most, because I'll mainly be using this machine to **make circuit
boards at home**. You'll hardly see it in any unboxing, because it's expensive even by Makera's
standards. Inside:

- **Ten blank boards**: five double-sided, copper on top and bottom, and five single-sided. Each one
  is enough for several small PCBs, not one board per blank.
- **Dedicated bits** for isolating the traces, and **drill bits** in several sizes for component holes.
- **Rivets** in various sizes, which connect the top to the bottom on double-sided boards: they do the
  job of vias.
- **UV solder mask** (the green coating on PCBs) with a **roller** to spread it.
- A small **UV curing box with a USB-C connector** to harden the mask.

The upshot is the whole process at home. No acids, no ferric chloride, no minimum order and weeks of
waiting: you start from a blank, the CNC cuts the traces, then drilling, then mask, and **a single
prototype** is perfectly doable.

## The ionizer module

Last but not least. When you machine plastic, the chips pick up static and **stick to everything**,
the walls of the machine included. The ionizer neutralises the charge and solves the problem. I'll
test it in practice like everything else.

## First impressions

Packaging and build are excellent. The number of accessories, though, is a bit daunting: every one of
them has to be understood before you can use it, on top of the machine itself, and **a CNC is not as
simple as a 3D printer**, not even this one. That's exactly what Makera is betting on with its
software, the thing that earned it the nickname "the Bambu Lab of CNCs": lowering the barrier for
people starting from zero, like me.

That's why I chose the Z1 as my first CNC rather than one of the cheap ones you find online. Whether
it keeps its promise we'll see in the next videos, with the machine running and the first jobs. In
the meantime I'm also working on a small piece of software to help people who only know 3D printing
get into CNC.
