The Snapmaker U1 has arrived, and this is the first real contact with the machine: box open, parts on the bench, step-by-step assembly and a first print in four colours.

## What's in the box

Building a toolchanger differs from a conventional printer in one substantial way: on top of the basic mechanics there are **the print heads**, each with its own docking system, plus the station where they park when not in use. That's the part that needs the most care during assembly, because the alignment between heads and station determines whether tool changes will work reliably for the months that follow.

## The build

Assembly is typical of semi-assembled machines: the base arrives built, the gantry has to be fitted, then covers, belts and wiring. Nothing impossible, but this isn't a printer you pull out of the box and switch on.

One practical piece of advice, true for any printer: **don't skip squaring the frame.** A gantry mounted out of square produces prints that look like they have a software calibration fault and that no profile will fix. Half an hour more during assembly saves ten hours of diagnosis later.

## The first four-colour print

The real test of a toolchanger isn't surface quality — it's **repeatability of the tool change**. On a multicolour print the machine performs hundreds of docking and undocking cycles. If even one fails halfway through the job, the part is lost.

That's why a first four-colour print tells you far more than a benchy: it stresses precisely the mechanism that separates this machine from a normal printer.

## The concrete advantage over an AMS

On a single-filament system with automatic changing, every colour change requires a purge: the nozzle has to push out the previous material before printing with the new one. On a print with many changes, the waste can exceed the weight of the part itself.

With four independent heads the problem doesn't exist: each filament has its own path, there's no mixing, and there's nothing to purge. That is the reason to choose a toolchanger.
