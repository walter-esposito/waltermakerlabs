An unboxing tells you what a machine is like on day one. After a month of long prints, constant tool changes and different materials, the picture is a different one &mdash; and that's the picture that matters if you're deciding whether to buy.

This isn't a lab review and there are no benchmarks: these are the things that only come out by using the machine every day.

## Why the first month is the period that counts

On a conventional printer, problems show up almost immediately: either it prints or it doesn't. On a toolchanger they don't. The faults that matter are **cumulative**, and they only appear at volume:

- a docking cycle that fails once in a thousand is invisible on day one, but on an eight-colour print with hundreds of changes it becomes statistically certain
- wear on the contacts and the mechanical couplings is measured in cycles, not hours
- the offset calibration between heads tends to drift, and you notice when two adjacent colours stop lining up

## What changes when you mix materials

Moving from a single material to different filaments across the heads is the moment the machine is genuinely tested: different temperatures, different shrinkage, different bed adhesion — all on the same plate, within the same print. That's a complexity a single-extruder printer never has to face.

## What to weigh up before buying

The right question isn't "is this a good printer", it's **"do I print enough multicolour work to justify a toolchanger?"** If the answer is no, a well-configured single-extruder machine will give you fewer headaches for the same result.

If you do regularly print with several colours or several materials, the maths changes: the filament you don't throw away in purges pays back part of the price difference, and that difference compounds with every print.
