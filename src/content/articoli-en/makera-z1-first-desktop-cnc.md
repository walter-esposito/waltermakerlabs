After years of 3D printing, I've taken the next step: I bought a **CNC**. The choice landed on the Makera Z1 Pro, backed on Kickstarter.

> **Disclosure:** this isn't sponsored. I paid for the machine myself, as a campaign backer. This is the genuine start of my route into CNC, not promotional content.

## Why a CNC after 3D printing

3D printing and milling don't compete: they do opposite things. Printing adds material layer by layer; a CNC removes it from a solid block. Everything else follows from that difference.

A CNC lets you work on things a printer can't touch:

- **aluminium** and other soft metals, for brackets and parts that have to carry real loads
- **PCBs** etched at home, with no chemical process
- **laser engraving**, with the dedicated module
- **prototypes** in final materials, not in test plastic

A milled aluminium part and a PLA-printed part aren't the same object in different materials: they're two categories of object with different uses.

## Why the Z1 specifically

The label that got attached to it — "the Bambu Lab of CNC" — describes the positioning well: a machine aiming to make milling accessible to people who don't come from a machine-shop background.

That's exactly the leap 3D printing made years ago. Desktop CNC stayed hostile territory for a long time: complicated software, cutting parameters you learn the hard way, machines that assume prior expertise. If the Z1 genuinely lowers that barrier, it opens milling up to a lot of makers who have stayed outside it.

## What's in the package

Besides the machine in Founder Edition trim: two MDF spoil boards, the rod for the 3D probe, a precision 6 mm collet and two 6 mm to 3.175 mm adapters for bits.

The spoil board deserves an explanation, because it means nothing to someone coming from 3D printing: it's a sacrificial panel that goes **underneath** the workpiece. When the cutter exits the material, it cuts into that instead of the machine bed. It's a consumable, and having two spare is right.

## What's coming next

Unboxing and first setup, home-made PCBs, aluminium work, laser tests, and the integration between CNC, 3D printing and robotics — which is, after all, why a machine like this ends up in a workshop that already has printers.
